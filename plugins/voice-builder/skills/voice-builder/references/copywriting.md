# Writing effective, on-brand copy for store sections and campaigns

The owner judges the copy by its words first. Correct facts in generic sale-speak is a failure: it reads like any
store's template. Every line should be something only this brand would write, that a shopper understands in one
glance, and that points to one next step.

## 1. Load the voice and the facts before writing a word

Read all of `BRAND-VOICE.md`, not just the three words:
- **the three words:** every line fits all three;
- **How it sounds:** sentence length, we/you, humour, how it handles urgency, and the bullet for this channel;
- **Signature phrases:** reuse their rhythm and attitude; quote one where it fits;
- **Do / don't pairs and words to avoid:** every "don't" is a hard ban.

From `BRAND.md`: Terminology (exact names and spellings), Claim limits (what can never be promised, the disclaimers),
the channels in use, and the product facts. Where BRAND-VOICE.md and BRAND.md disagree, BRAND.md wins.

Facts come only from the owner, the brand files or the live site. Never invent a statistic, a review, a testimonial,
a result, an award, a deadline or scarcity ("only 12 left", "while supplies last" unless the owner says so).

## 2. Three directions, then pick

Where there is a real choice (headlines, supporting lines, subject lines, captions, the hook of a long piece), write
**three versions with clearly different angles**:
1. **The brand's wink:** its humour or attitude applied to the offer or product.
2. **Product-led:** the product, the ritual or the problem it solves, then the offer.
3. **Plain offer:** the cleanest statement of the deal or benefit, still in the brand's rhythm.

Recommend one; show the other two as one-line alternatives. The same facts, dates and link appear in all three. The
plain-offer version is exempt from the swap test in section 8 (it is plain by design) but is recommended only when it
carries a brand phrase or a brand-specific detail.

- **Where the humour lives.** If BRAND-VOICE.md says the brand's humour comes from characters, names or openers and not
  from the deal, the wink goes in the opener, headline or subject line, carried by a character or a signature phrase,
  and the offer itself stays plain. With no character or phrase to use, replace the wink with a **ritual-led** version.
- **Lines that are not selling** (a closing notice, an apology, a thank-you, an error): replace product-led with
  **plain with the reason**, and the wink with a **warm** version. Never pad a direction that has nothing to lead with.
- **One clear option.** Error messages, "Add to cart" and field labels have one right answer: give one line and say so.

## 3. What each surface is for, and how long it is

Write for the job of the surface, and lead with what the shopper gets, not with the brand.

| Surface | Job | Length and shape |
|---|---|---|
| Announcement bar | Say the offer and when, on every page | One line, about 60 characters on a phone (up to 65 with a signature opener); offer, then the end date |
| Hero headline | Stop the scroll with the one idea | 2 to 5 words; a phrase the brand would say, not a slogan template |
| Hero supporting line | The offer, the dates, the reason to care | One sentence, about 12 words |
| Hero small print | Carry what the line can't: end time and zone, exclusions, ship date | One short line under the button, same wording as the product page |
| Buttons | Name the next step | 2 or 3 specific words ("Shop the sale", "Get yours"); never "Learn more" or "Click here" |
| Product title and subtitle | Say what it is and who it is for | The brand's exact product name, then one plain line |
| Product description | Answer "what is it, why this one, how do I use it" | A one-sentence hook, 3 to 5 benefit lines each with a concrete detail (size, material, use), then care or shipping facts |
| Collection intro | Orient and point to a starting place | 1 to 2 sentences; name what unites the products |
| About / story | Make the brand a person | 3 short paragraphs: why it exists, what it believes, what it makes; use the brand's own words from BRAND.md Story |
| FAQ answers | Remove the last doubt | Answer in the first sentence, then one line of detail; use the shopper's wording for the question |
| Email subject and preview | Earn the open | Subject under 45 characters; the preview line adds, never repeats |
| Email body | One idea, one button | Hook, two short paragraphs at most, one button |
| Social and ad captions | Fit the platform | Instagram: looser, image-led. A platform that restricts the category: follow the brand's own cautious copy set and its Claim limits; never reuse another channel's wording |
| Page title and meta description | Be found and clicked | Title under 60 characters with the product or topic first; description 140 to 155 characters ending in a reason to click |
| Countdown and "ended" message | Create a real deadline, then close it | State the exact end; the ended message thanks and points to the next step |

**Where the parts of a deal go.** The offer and its end date fit the bar and hero. The end **time and time zone**,
exclusions, ship date and "estimated" wording go where there is room (the offer section, the product description, the
email and the FAQ) and must be the same everywhere they appear. If a line can't hold them, drop the ship date first,
then the exclusions, and never the end date. Anything dropped from a short surface must appear on the page it links to.

Buttons, forms, errors, cart, checkout and empty states follow section 5. SMS, TikTok or any channel the brand does
not use is never written unless BRAND.md lists it.

## 4. What makes copy effective

- **Every piece has one job:** inform, reassure, persuade or guide to the next step. Name it before writing.
- **Use the customer's own words.** "Doesn't dry out my skin" beats "advanced hydration technology". Take phrases from
  real reviews, support questions and survey answers, with their source, never invented.
- **Benefits before features.** "Stays cold for 24 hours" first, "double-wall vacuum insulation" as the support.
- **Answer the objections where they arise:** sizing, shipping, returns, materials, "will this work for me?" go in
  bullets, a short FAQ, or one line near the Add to Cart button.
- **Specific proof beats adjectives.** A number, a real review, a certification, a guarantee or a press mention the
  owner can show is stronger than "premium" or "high-quality". No proof available: leave it out.
- **Scannable product pages:** a headline with the main benefit, a short description, key bullets, then details
  (materials, care, specs).
- **Match the funnel stage.** Ads: one hook, one offer. Landing pages: continue the exact promise of the ad. Product
  pages: persuade and remove doubt. Emails: a strong subject, one idea, one button. Abandoned-cart emails: remove
  friction (a question answered, shipping or returns stated), never nag.
- **One idea per surface and one action.** A hero with an offer, a product pitch and a story does none of them.
- **Specific beats grand.** A concrete detail ("fits in a palm", "ends 11:59 pm Dec 1") is stronger than an adjective
  ("amazing", "premium"). Replace every adjective with the fact behind it, or cut it.
- **Lead with the shopper.** The shopper's gain or problem comes before the brand's pride.
- **Say the deal plainly.** Offer, what it applies to, exclusions, end date and time zone, in that order, never hidden
  across the set as a whole (short surfaces carry the offer and end date; section 3 says where the rest goes).
  Say "sitewide", not "everything", when gift cards or items are excluded.
- **Real urgency only.** A true end date is urgency. Invented scarcity is a claim, and a violation of Claim limits.
- **Rhythm is voice.** Match the brand's sentence length and where it puts the joke or the pause. Read the signature
  phrases aloud and write in that cadence.
- **Names.** Use the brand's exact names for its own things (BRAND.md Terminology), including words it forbids ("don't
  call it a subscription") and the right name for a list versus a product with a similar name.
- **Dates read the way shoppers read them:** "Nov 27 to Dec 1", never "11/27". "Ends Dec 1" means the end of Dec 1.

## 5. Interface microcopy (buttons, forms, cart, checkout, errors)

Persuasion copy is brand-led and expressive; interface copy guides and removes friction, so it is neutral and
functional. The brand's voice shows in word choice and warmth, never at the cost of clarity.

- **Clear before clever.** A shopper never guesses what a button does: "Add to cart", not "Let's go!".
- **Say what happens next.** "Place order", "Save address", "Send my link", never "Submit".
- **Reassure at friction points,** in one short line: "Free returns within 30 days" by the buy button, "We'll never
  share your email" by a signup field. Only if the statement is true; take it from BRAND.md or the store's policies.
- **Helpful errors:** say what went wrong and how to fix it ("Enter an email like name@example.com"), never "Invalid
  input". No blame, no jargon, no exclamation marks.
- **One name for one thing.** Use BRAND.md's Terminology, which wins over a generic label when the label stays clear
  ("Pre-order" over "Add to cart" on a pre-order item), and stay consistent: not "cart", "bag" and "basket", not "sign
  in" and "log in". Check the whole set before delivering.
- **Short.** As brief as possible without losing meaning.
- **Design the edge cases:** empty cart, out of stock, loading, failed payment, 404, order confirmation. Each should
  say what happened and give a next step (a link to the collection, a back-in-stock signup).
- **Accessible:** plain language, descriptive link text ("View sizing guide", never "Click here"; platform conventions
  like "Link in bio" on Instagram are fine), meaningful alt text
  and form labels, and nothing that depends on colour or an icon alone to be understood.

## 6. Never write

- Generic sale-speak: "Don't miss out", "Shop now and save", "Limited time only", "Huge savings", "Treat yourself",
  "You deserve it", "Biggest sale of the year" (unless true and sourced).
- Filler verbs and AI tells: "unlock", "elevate", "indulge", "discover", "curated", "game-changer", "level up",
  "seamless", "in today's world".
- Stacked exclamation marks, all caps beyond what the brand already uses, emoji the brand does not use, em dashes in
  customer copy.
- Anything BRAND-VOICE.md lists under words or habits to avoid, and anything Claim limits forbids (for example health
  or outcome claims, comparisons with a named competitor, "approved" or "cleared" wording).

## 7. A campaign or a set of sections together

Write the whole set at once so every surface tells one story: the bar, hero, supporting line, button, page sections,
email and captions all carry the same offer, dates and link. Vary the angle between surfaces only in how it is said,
never in what is promised. Check once across the set that no surface says something another contradicts (for example
"35% off" in the hero and "free shipping" alone in the bar).

## 8. Check every line before showing it

For each line, and rewrite any that fails:
1. **Swap test:** put a competitor's name on it. If it still works, it is too generic: add the brand's product,
   ritual, attitude or one of its phrases.
2. **Three words:** does it fit each of the brand's three words?
3. **Do / don't:** does it break any "don't" pair?
4. **Facts and claims:** do the offer, dates, exclusions, names and link match the brief (after the source check in
   SKILL.md C1) exactly, and does anything promise more than Claim limits allow? Judge completeness across the set (the
   time zone, exclusions and ship date appear on the page that has room), not line by line. A fact still to be confirmed
   is marked `[confirm: …]`, not failed.
5. **One idea, one job, one action:** can a shopper say what it wants from them after one read?
6. **Customer words and proof:** does it use the customer's own wording where we have it, and is every proof point real
   and sourced? With no customer words found, mark this check "not applicable, flagged" for the set.
7. **Accessible and consistent:** plain language, descriptive link text, the same name for the same thing everywhere.
8. **Read aloud:** does it sound like the owner talking, or like an ad template?

Under the copy, add one line per recommended line saying which signature phrase or voice rule it leans on, so the
owner sees why it is on-brand.
