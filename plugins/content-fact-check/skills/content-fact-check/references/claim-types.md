# Claim types, risk tiers and wording rules

Contents: 1. Risk tiers · 2. Claim categories · 3. Regulatory-status wording (US) · 4. Other markets · 5. Pregnancy and safety · 6. Common fixes

## 1. Risk tiers

| Tier | Meaning | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Regulatory status, medical or treatment claims, safety (including pregnancy and breastfeeding), ingredient safety or concentration, "clinically proven" or efficacy figures | Check every one against a primary source |
| **Medium** | Statistics, performance and comparison claims, sustainability or origin claims, certifications | Check all that carry numbers or named bodies; sample the rest |
| **Low** | General explanatory facts, widely documented basics | Read for plain errors; research only if something looks wrong |

## 2. Claim categories

| Category | Examples | What to establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulatory status** | "FDA approved", "FDA cleared", "certified", "registered", "licensed", "meets ISO…" | Which body, which exact status, for which product and indication, current as of today |
| **Medical and treatment** | "treats…", "reduces the risk of…", "heals", "safe and effective", "clinically proven" | The indication, the evidence, whether it's an approved use, whether the wording implies a disease claim |
| **Ingredient** | "contains X%", "free of parabens", "X is non-toxic", "X penetrates the skin" | The correct ingredient name, the concentration, what the evidence says about the effect, any regulatory limits |
| **Pregnancy, breastfeeding, children** | "safe during pregnancy", "not recommended while nursing" | Authoritative guidance for that specific product or ingredient |
| **Safety** | "non-toxic", "hypoallergenic", "no side effects" | The standard the term actually meets (many have none) |
| **Statistics and studies** | "9 out of 10 users", "a 2021 study found…" | The original source, sample, date, and whether the post states it correctly |
| **Performance and comparison** | "lasts 3x longer", "the best", "results in 7 days" | Substantiation the brand holds (FTC-style standard: competent and reliable evidence) |
| **Sustainability and origin** | "eco-friendly", "natural", "organic", "made in…" | The certification or definition behind the word; whether it's defined or just marketing |

## 3. Regulatory-status wording (US)

The word matters. Check the exact term against the regulator's record, and don't let one word stand in for another.

- **Drugs and biologics** (including prescription injectables): the FDA **approves** them, for specific **indications**. "Approved" applies to the product and its listed uses, not to every use. Using it beyond the labeled indication is **off-label**. Check the label for indications, populations and warnings.
- **Medical devices:**
  - Many Class II devices are **cleared** (510(k), substantial equivalence), not "approved".
  - Class III devices are **approved** through premarket approval (PMA).
  - Some novel low-to-moderate-risk devices are **granted** via De Novo.
  - Clearance and approval are tied to a specific **intended use**. "FDA-cleared" does not mean cleared for every treatment or area the post mentions.
  - A device's cleared use can be narrower than how it is marketed. Compare the post's use claims with the cleared indications.
- **Cosmetics:** the FDA does **not approve** cosmetic products or their ingredients (color additives are the exception). "FDA approved" on a cosmetic is inaccurate. Statements about facility registration or product listing are not approval and shouldn't be worded as if they were. Cosmetics can't claim to treat or prevent disease or change the body's structure or function; those are drug claims.
- **Dietary supplements:** not FDA-approved. Structure/function claims need the FDA disclaimer and must not claim to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent a disease.
- **Sunscreens and some skin products** are regulated as over-the-counter drugs: check the monograph or labeling rules before claiming SPF or active-ingredient effects.
- **"FDA registered" / "FDA-registered facility"** means an establishment registered with the agency. It says nothing about the safety or effectiveness of its products. Flag any wording that lets a reader assume otherwise.
- **"Clinically proven" / "clinically tested" / "dermatologist recommended"**: need actual studies or documentation of who recommended it, on which product, with what result. "Clinically tested" and "clinically proven" are not the same claim.
- **Disease claims:** wording like "treats acne", "cures", "prevents infection" turns marketing copy into a drug or device claim. Flag for expert sign-off.
- **Before/after and testimonials:** results must be typical or clearly qualified; endorsements must disclose material connections.

## 4. Other markets

Rules and terms differ by country. Identify the market (from the brand file or the content) and use that market's regulator and wording: for example the EU (Cosmetics Regulation, Medical Device Regulation and CE marking, EMA for medicines), the UK (MHRA), Canada (Health Canada), Australia (TGA), the Philippines (FDA Philippines). A status in one market says nothing about another. If the content makes a US claim to an international audience, or the reverse, flag the mismatch. `sources.md` lists where to check.

## 5. Pregnancy, breastfeeding and safety

- Never leave a statement that something is safe or unsafe in pregnancy or breastfeeding unless an authoritative source states it for that exact product or ingredient and use.
- Prefer neutral, accurate wording ("Ask your healthcare provider before using this during pregnancy") over a verdict, unless the source clearly supports a stronger statement.
- Verdict for any pregnancy, breastfeeding or pediatric safety claim that isn't directly supported by the product's official label or a professional body's guidance: **Needs expert sign-off**.
- "Non-toxic", "safe for everyone" and "hypoallergenic" have no standard meaning in many markets. Soften or define.

## 6. Common fixes

| Problem | Typical fix |
|---|---|
| "FDA-approved treatment" for a cleared device | "[Device] is FDA-cleared for [exact cleared use]" (only if the record says so), or remove the status |
| "FDA approved" on a cosmetic or supplement | Remove; describe what the product is and does within permitted claims |
| Status claim with no product or indication | Name the product and the indication, or drop |
| "Clinically proven to…" with no study | Cite the study with the correct wording, or "formulated to…" / remove |
| Absolute claims ("safe for everyone", "no side effects", "permanent results") | Qualify ("most people", "may", "results vary") or remove |
| Number with no source | Cite the original source, correct the figure, or remove |
| Ingredient claim stated more strongly than the evidence | Match the evidence ("studies suggest…"), cite it |
| Disease claim in marketing copy | Reword to a permitted claim, or get expert/legal sign-off |
